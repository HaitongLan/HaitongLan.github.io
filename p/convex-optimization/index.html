Convex Sets and Cones

From lines to line segments

Definition 1 (Affine Set, 仿射集). A set $C\subseteq\mathbb R^n$ is affine if, for any $x_1,x_2\in C$ and $\theta\in\mathbb R$,

$$ \theta x_1+(1-\theta)x_2\in C. $$

Allowing every real $\theta$ means that the entire line through $x_1$ and $x_2$ lies in $C$. If we restrict $\theta$ to $[0,1]$, only the line segment between the two points is required to stay in the set. This gives the central geometric notion below.

Definition 2 (Convex Set, 凸集). A set $C\subseteq\mathbb R^n$ is convex if, for any $x_1,x_2\in C$ and $\theta\in[0,1]$,

$$ \theta x_1+(1-\theta)x_2\in C. $$

Every affine set is therefore convex, but the converse is generally false. A norm ball is convex, while its boundary is generally not: a chord joining two boundary points usually passes through the interior.

An ellipsoid is an affine image of a Euclidean ball. If $A$ is nonsingular and $P=AA^\top\succ0$, then

$$ \mathcal E=\lbrace x_c+Au:\|u\|_2\leq1\rbrace =\lbrace x:(x-x_c)^\top P^{-1}(x-x_c)\leq1\rbrace. $$

The second description follows from $u=A^{-1}(x-x_c)$ and $\|u\|_2^2=(x-x_c)^\top(AA^\top)^{-1}(x-x_c)$.

Combining points

Definition 3 (Convex Combination, 凸组合). A convex combination of $x_1,\ldots,x_k$ is

$$ x=\sum_{i=1}^k\theta_i x_i, \qquad \theta_i\geq0, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^k\theta_i=1. $$

The nonnegative weights keep the result between the points, while the unit-sum condition makes the construction invariant under translations. Repeatedly applying the two-point definition shows that a set is convex if and only if it contains every finite convex combination of its points.

Definition 4 (Convex Hull, 凸包). The convex hull of $C$ is

$$ \operatorname{conv} C=\left\lbrace\sum_{i=1}^k\theta_i x_i: x_i\in C,\ \theta_i\geq0,\ \sum_{i=1}^k\theta_i=1\right\rbrace. $$

It is the smallest convex set containing $C$: the displayed set is convex and contains $C$, while every convex set containing $C$ must contain all such combinations.

If the unit-sum condition is removed, scaling becomes possible and we obtain a cone.

Definition 5 (Conic Combination, 锥组合). A conic combination is

$$ x=\sum_{i=1}^k\theta_i x_i, \qquad \theta_i\geq0. $$

Definition 6 (Convex Cone, 凸锥). A set $K$ is a convex cone if

$$ x_1,x_2\in K,\quad \theta_1,\theta_2\geq0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta_1x_1+\theta_2x_2\in K. $$

Typical examples are $\mathbb R_+^n$, the positive-semidefinite cone $\mathbb S_+^n$, and the second-order cone

$$ \mathcal Q^{n+1}=\lbrace(x,t)\in\mathbb R^{n+1}:\|x\|_2\leq t\rbrace. $$

Operations that preserve convexity

Proposition 7. Convexity is preserved by the following operations.

  • Intersection. If every $C_\alpha$ is convex, then $\bigcap_\alpha C_\alpha$ is convex.

  • Affine image. If $C$ is convex and $f(x)=Ax+b$, then $f(C)$ is convex.

  • Affine inverse image. If $C$ is convex, then $\lbrace x:Ax+b\in C\rbrace$ is convex.

  • Perspective mapping. The image of a convex set contained in $\lbrace(x,t):t>0\rbrace$ under $P(x,t)=x/t$ is convex.

  • Linear-fractional mapping. On $c^\top x+d>0$, the image of a convex set under

    $$ f(x)=\frac{Ax+b}{c^\top x+d} $$

    is convex.

The first three facts follow directly by taking a convex combination and checking membership. For perspective maps, if $u_i=x_i/t_i$ and $0\leq\theta\leq1$, then

$$ \theta u_1+(1-\theta)u_2 =\frac{\lambda x_1+(1-\lambda)x_2}{\lambda t_1+(1-\lambda)t_2}, $$

where

$$ \lambda=\frac{\theta/t_1}{\theta/t_1+(1-\theta)/t_2}\in[0,1]. $$

Thus the left-hand side is the perspective image of a convex combination of the original points. A linear-fractional map is an affine map followed by a perspective map, so the final claim follows from the same argument.

As an important example,

$$ \lbrace x:\|Ax+b\|_2\leq c^\top x+d\rbrace $$

is convex: $x\mapsto(Ax+b,c^\top x+d)$ is affine, and $\lbrace(u,t):\|u\|_2\leq t\rbrace$ is the second-order cone. The set above is therefore an affine inverse image of a convex cone.

Proper cones and generalized inequalities

Motivation: what should “larger” mean for a vector? For real numbers, $a\leq b$ means exactly that $b-a\in\mathbb R_+$. To generalize this idea, choose a set $K$ of directions that count as nonnegative, and declare $x\preceq_K y$ when $y-x\in K$. The question is then: what properties of $K$ make this relation behave like an inequality?

Definition 8 (Proper Cone, 正则锥). A cone $K$ is proper if it is closed, convex, pointed, and has nonempty interior:

$$ K\cap(-K)=\lbrace0\rbrace, \qquad \operatorname{int}K\neq\varnothing. $$

Each condition has a specific role. For a nonempty convex cone, $0\in K$, so $x\preceq_K x$ (reflexivity). Closure under addition gives transitivity: if $y-x\in K$ and $z-y\in K$, then $z-x=(z-y)+(y-x)\in K$. Pointedness gives antisymmetry: if both $y-x$ and $x-y$ lie in $K$, then $y-x=0$, so $x=y$.

The cone structure also makes the order compatible with nonnegative scaling and addition. For example,

$$ x\preceq_K y\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha x+z\preceq_K\alpha y+z, \qquad \alpha\geq0. $$

Why require a proper cone, rather than just a pointed convex cone? A nonempty pointed convex cone already defines a partial order. Closedness and nonempty interior are extra regularity conditions useful in optimization: closedness ensures inequalities survive limits, while nonempty interior makes strict inequalities possible in the ambient space and stable under sufficiently small perturbations.

For example, $K=\lbrace(t,0):t\geq0\rbrace\subset\mathbb R^2$ defines a partial order, but has empty interior in $\mathbb R^2$. Consequently, the interior-based strict inequality below would never hold. Conversely, a cone containing a line fails antisymmetry: a nonzero direction on that line would count as both nonnegative and nonpositive. Thus “proper” packages the order properties together with the regularity needed for strict feasibility and duality; it is not the minimal assumption for every possible partial order.

Definition 9 (Generalized Inequality, 广义不等式). A proper cone $K$ induces the partial order

$$ x\preceq_K y\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y-x\in K, $$

and its strict version

$$ x\prec_K y\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y-x\in\operatorname{int}K. $$

For $K=\mathbb R_+^n$, this is componentwise inequality. For $K=\mathbb S_+^n$,

$$ A\preceq B\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad B-A\succeq0, $$

which is the Loewner order on symmetric matrices. This viewpoint lets scalar, vector, and matrix inequalities share one language. The order need not be total: under componentwise order, $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ are incomparable. Also, $x\prec_K y$ means that $y-x$ lies in the interior, not merely that $x\preceq_K y$ and $x\neq y$.

Dual cones and dual norms

Motivation: which scalar measurements respect this order? Once $K$ tells us which vectors are nonnegative, we may want to turn a vector inequality into an ordinary scalar inequality. Every linear functional on $\mathbb R^n$ has the form $x\mapsto y^\top x$. For this measurement to preserve the order, we need

$$ u\preceq_K v\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y^\top u\leq y^\top v. $$

Since $v-u\in K$, this is equivalent to requiring $y^\top x\geq0$ for every $x\in K$. Collecting exactly those admissible measurements gives the following definition.

Definition 10 (Dual Cone, 对偶锥). The dual cone of $K$ is

$$ K^*=\lbrace y:y^\top x\geq0\text{ for every }x\in K\rbrace. $$

Thus $K$ specifies positive directions, $x\preceq_K y$ compares vectors using those directions, and $K^*$ collects the linear measurements that are nonnegative on them. The proper cone $K$ itself induces the generalized inequality; constructing $K^*$ is not a prerequisite. In fact, the dual cone can be defined even when $K$ is not proper. When $K$ is proper, its dual is also proper and can additionally induce its own order.

For example, if $K=\mathbb R_+^n$, then all weights in an order-preserving linear measurement must be nonnegative. A negative weight $y_i$ would assign a negative value to the nonnegative coordinate vector $e_i$. Hence $(\mathbb R_+^n)^*=\mathbb R_+^n$: the orthant is self-dual, although a general cone need not be.

Why this becomes useful in constrained optimization. Consider a minimization problem with a cone inequality

$$ \min_x f_0(x)\qquad\text{subject to }g(x)\preceq_K0. $$

Feasibility means $-g(x)\in K$. We want a scalar Lagrangian $L(x,\lambda)=f_0(x)+\lambda^\top g(x)$ whose constraint term is nonpositive at every feasible point. This gives a lower bound on the feasible objective. The definition of the dual cone supplies exactly the required sign condition:

$$ \lambda\in K^*,\quad -g(x)\in K \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda^\top g(x)\leq0. $$

Consequently, for every feasible $x$,

$$ q(\lambda):=\inf_z L(z,\lambda) \leq L(x,\lambda)\leq f_0(x), \qquad \lambda\in K^*. $$

Here the infimum is over the underlying domain of the objective and constraint functions. This is the lower-bound mechanism of Lagrange duality: the primal constraint uses $K$, and the dual multiplier belongs to $K^*$. For the ordinary scalar constraint $g(x)\leq0$, both cones are $\mathbb R_+$, recovering the familiar multiplier condition $\lambda\geq0$. This lower-bound argument does not itself require convexity or assert that the best bound equals the primal optimum.

The next definitions quantify the size of linear measurements. This will let us compute the dual of a norm cone explicitly.

Definition 11 ($\ell_p$ Norm, $\ell_p$ 范数). For $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\in\mathbb R^n$ and $1\leq p<\infty$, the $\ell_p$ norm is

$$ \|x\|_p=\left(\sum_{i=1}^n|x_i|^p\right)^{1/p}. $$

For $p=\infty$, it is defined by

$$ \|x\|_\infty=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|x_i|. $$

Important special cases are the $\ell_1$ norm $\|x\|_1=\sum_i|x_i|$, the Euclidean norm $\|x\|_2=(\sum_i x_i^2)^{1/2}$, and the maximum norm $\|x\|_\infty=\max_i|x_i|$. Strictly speaking, $\ell_p$ denotes the norm of a finite-dimensional vector or a sequence; the corresponding norm for a measurable function $f$ is the $L_p$ norm

$$ \|f\|_{L_p}=\left(\int |f(t)|^p\,dt\right)^{1/p}, \qquad \|f\|_{L_\infty}=\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_t|f(t)|. $$

Unless stated otherwise, this notebook uses $\|\cdot\|_p$ for the finite-dimensional $\ell_p$ norm.

Definition 12 (Dual Norm, 对偶范数). The dual norm is

$$ \|y\|_*=\sup_{\|x\|\leq1}y^\top x. $$

It immediately yields the generalized Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $y^\top x\leq\|y\|_*\|x\|$. For conjugate exponents satisfying $1/p+1/q=1$, Hölder’s inequality gives

$$ \|\cdot\|_p^*=\|\cdot\|_q. $$

In particular, $\|\cdot\|_2$ is self-dual, while $\|\cdot\|_1^*=\|\cdot\|_\infty$.

Proposition 13 (Norm-Cone Duality). For

$$ K=\lbrace(x,t):\|x\|\leq t\rbrace, $$

the dual cone is

$$ K^*=\lbrace(y,s):\|y\|_*\leq s\rbrace. $$

Indeed, $(y,s)\in K^*$ precisely when $y^\top x+st\geq0$ for all $\|x\|\leq t$. After scaling to $t=1$, the smallest possible value is $s-\|y\|_*$. It is nonnegative exactly when $\|y\|_*\leq s$. Because the Euclidean norm is self-dual, the second-order cone is also self-dual.

Theorem 14 (Dual of a Proper Cone). If $K$ is a proper cone, then $K^*$ is also a proper cone. For closed convex cones,

$$ K\text{ is pointed}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \operatorname{int}K^*\neq\varnothing, $$ $$ \operatorname{int}K\neq\varnothing\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad K^*\text{ is pointed}. $$

These relations explain why the four conditions defining a proper cone are preserved under duality.

Separation as a certificate

Definition 15 (Separating Hyperplane, 分离超平面). A hyperplane

$$ H=\lbrace x:a^\top x=b\rbrace,\qquad a\neq0, $$

separates $C$ and $D$ if

$$ a^\top x\leq b\leq a^\top y \qquad\text{for every }x\in C\text{ and }y\in D. $$

Theorem 16 (Separating Hyperplane Theorem, 分离超平面定理). If $C,D\subseteq\mathbb R^n$ are nonempty, disjoint, convex sets, then there exist $a\neq0$ and $b$ that separate them. Additional assumptions, such as one set being open or one set being compact while the other is closed, give strict or strong forms of separation.

Separation turns geometric nonintersection into a linear certificate. Separating a point from a convex feasible set, for example, produces an inequality witnessing infeasibility. The same mechanism later leads to supporting hyperplanes, optimality conditions, Farkas’ lemma, and Lagrange duality.

The logical chain of this section is

$$ \text{convex combinations} \longrightarrow \text{convex sets and cones} \longrightarrow \text{cone-induced orders} \longrightarrow \text{dual cones} \longrightarrow \text{separation certificates}. $$
