An ongoing study notebook on computational optimal transport. The main idea in these notes is to understand how deterministic transport maps lead naturally to couplings and the Kantorovich relaxation.

Theoretical Foundations

Monge Problem

Motivation: suppose two discrete measures contain the same total mass. A deterministic transport assigns the mass at each source point to exactly one target point. In the uniform case, this reduces to a matching problem.

Definition 1 (Discrete Measure, 离散测度). A discrete probability measure on points $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ is $$ \alpha=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\delta_{x_i}, \qquad a_i\geq0, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^n a_i=1. $$ Similarly, $$ \beta=\sum_{j=1}^m b_j\delta_{y_j}. $$ The vectors $a=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)^\top$ and $b=(b_1,\ldots,b_m)^\top$ record the masses, while the Dirac measures record their locations.

Definition 2 (Discrete Monge Transport). A deterministic transport assigns each source point $x_i$ to one target point, $$ T(x_i)=y_{\sigma(i)}. $$ For uniform measures with $n=m$ and $a=b=\mathbf 1_n/n$, this is equivalent to a permutation $\sigma\in\operatorname{Perm}(n)$. If $$ C_{ij}=c(x_i,y_j), $$ the matching problem is $$ \min_{\sigma\in\operatorname{Perm}(n)} \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n C_{i,\sigma(i)}. $$

The discrete formulation suggests the general question: how should a map $T$ act on an entire measure?

Definition 3 (Push-forward Measure, 前推测度). Let $T:\mathcal X\to\mathcal Y$ be measurable and let $\alpha$ be a measure on $\mathcal X$. The push-forward of $\alpha$ by $T$ is the measure $T_\#\alpha$ on $\mathcal Y$ defined by $$ (T_\#\alpha)(B) = \alpha(T^{-1}(B)), \qquad B\subseteq\mathcal Y. $$ Thus $T$ moves points, while $T_\#$ moves measures: $$ T:\mathcal X\to\mathcal Y, \qquad T_\#:\mathcal M_+(\mathcal X)\to\mathcal M_+(\mathcal Y). $$

The definition means that the mass observed in $B\subseteq\mathcal Y$ after transport is exactly the mass that originally lay in its preimage $T^{-1}(B)$.

Proposition 4 (Integration Formula). For any integrable function $f:\mathcal Y\to\mathbb R$, $$ \int_{\mathcal Y} f(y)\,d(T_\#\alpha)(y) = \int_{\mathcal X} f(T(x))\,d\alpha(x). $$

Remark 5 (Push-forward of Densities). Suppose $\mathcal X=\mathcal Y=\mathbb R^d$, $T$ is smooth and bijective, and $$ d\alpha(x)=\rho_\alpha(x)\,dx, \qquad d\beta(y)=\rho_\beta(y)\,dy, \qquad \beta=T_\#\alpha. $$ Then the change-of-variables formula gives $$ \rho_\alpha(x) = \rho_\beta(T(x)) \left|\det DT(x)\right|. $$ Equivalently, $$ \rho_\beta(y) = \frac{\rho_\alpha(T^{-1}(y))} {\left|\det DT(T^{-1}(y))\right|}. $$ The Jacobian appears because the map changes local volume as well as location.

Remark 6 (Measures and Random Variables, 测度与随机变量). A random variable is a measurable map $$ X:(\Omega,\mathcal F,P)\to\mathcal X. $$ Its distribution is precisely the push-forward of $P$ by $X$: $$ \alpha=X_\#P. $$ Indeed, $$ \alpha(A) = P(X\in A) = P(X^{-1}(A)). $$ If $T:\mathcal X\to\mathcal Y$ and $$ Y=T(X), $$ then $$ \operatorname{Law}(Y) = T_\#\alpha = T_\#(X_\#P) = (T\circ X)_\#P. $$ Hence $$ X\sim\alpha, \qquad T_\#\alpha=\beta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T(X)\sim\beta. $$ This is the probabilistic meaning of transport: applying the deterministic map $T$ to samples from $\alpha$ produces samples from $\beta$.

Definition 7 (Pull-back Operator, 后拉算子). Let $T:\mathcal X\to\mathcal Y$ and let $f:\mathcal Y\to\mathbb R$. The pull-back of $f$ by $T$ is $$ T^\# f = f\circ T, $$ that is, $$ (T^\#f)(x)=f(T(x)). $$

Remark 8 (Push-forward vs. Pull-back). The two operations act on different objects and in opposite directions: $$ T_\#: \mathcal M_+(\mathcal X) \to \mathcal M_+(\mathcal Y), $$ while $$ T^\#: \{f:\mathcal Y\to\mathbb R\} \to \{f:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R\}. $$ Measures are pushed forward, while functions are naturally pulled back. Their relation is $$ \int_{\mathcal Y} f\,d(T_\#\alpha) = \int_{\mathcal X} T^\#f\,d\alpha. $$ For densities, $$ \rho_\alpha = |\det DT|\,T^\#\rho_\beta, $$ so the density is not obtained by a naive pull-back alone.

Definition 9 (Monge Problem). Given probability measures $\alpha$ on $\mathcal X$ and $\beta$ on $\mathcal Y$, and a cost $c:\mathcal X\times\mathcal Y\to\mathbb R$, the Monge problem is $$ \inf_{T:\,T_\#\alpha=\beta} \int_{\mathcal X} c(x,T(x))\,d\alpha(x). $$ The constraint $T_\#\alpha=\beta$ requires $T$ to transport the source measure exactly onto the target measure.

The essential restriction is determinism: for each $x$, all mass located at $x$ must be sent to the single point $T(x)$. Consequently, a Monge map need not exist. For example, $$ \alpha=\delta_0, \qquad \beta=\frac12\delta_{-1}+\frac12\delta_1 $$ cannot be connected by any deterministic map, because $T_\#\delta_0$ is always a single Dirac measure.

Kantorovich Relaxation

Why relax Monge?

Motivation: the Monge formulation has two structural difficulties. First, the deterministic constraint forbids mass splitting and can make the feasible set empty. Second, optimization over maps satisfying $T_\#\alpha=\beta$ has poor convex structure. Kantorovich replaces a deterministic map by a coupling, allowing a source mass to be split among several targets and turning the problem into linear optimization over a convex set.

Definition 1 (Admissible Coupling, 可行耦合). For discrete measures $$ \alpha=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\delta_{x_i}, \qquad \beta=\sum_{j=1}^m b_j\delta_{y_j}, $$ an admissible coupling is a nonnegative matrix $$ P\in\mathbb R_+^{n\times m} $$ satisfying $$ P\mathbf1_m=a, \qquad P^\top\mathbf1_n=b. $$ The admissible set is $$ U(a,b) = \left\{ P\in\mathbb R_+^{n\times m}: P\mathbf1_m=a,\; P^\top\mathbf1_n=b \right\}. $$ Here $P_{ij}$ is the amount of mass transported from $x_i$ to $y_j$.

The constraints have a direct conservation interpretation: $$ \sum_j P_{ij}=a_i, \qquad \sum_i P_{ij}=b_j. $$

Definition 2 (Discrete Kantorovich Problem). Let $$ C_{ij}=c(x_i,y_j). $$ The discrete Kantorovich problem is $$ L_C(a,b) = \min_{P\in U(a,b)} \langle C,P\rangle = \min_{P\in U(a,b)} \sum_{i,j} C_{ij}P_{ij}. $$ Unlike a permutation, a row of $P$ may contain several positive entries, so the mass at one source point may be split among several targets.

Birkhoff polytope and matching.

Definition 3 (Birkhoff Polytope). The Birkhoff polytope is the set of doubly stochastic matrices $$ \mathcal B_n = \left\{ Q\in\mathbb R_+^{n\times n}: Q\mathbf1_n=\mathbf1_n,\; Q^\top\mathbf1_n=\mathbf1_n \right\}. $$ For uniform measures, $$ a=b=\frac{\mathbf1_n}{n}, $$ the admissible set satisfies $$ U\left(\frac{\mathbf1_n}{n},\frac{\mathbf1_n}{n}\right) = \frac1n\mathcal B_n. $$

Theorem 4 (Birkhoff–von Neumann). Every doubly stochastic matrix is a convex combination of permutation matrices: $$ Q = \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k P_{\sigma_k}, \qquad \lambda_k\geq0, \qquad \sum_{k=1}^K\lambda_k=1. $$ Equivalently, the extreme points of $\mathcal B_n$ are exactly the permutation matrices.

Remark 5 (Tightness for Assignment Problems). Although Kantorovich couplings allow mass splitting, the Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem shows that every feasible plan for uniform measures is a convex combination of permutation couplings. Because the objective is linear, an optimum is attained at a permutation coupling; thus the relaxation has the same value as the matching problem.

Couplings as joint distributions.

The matrix formulation is only the discrete representation of a more general object. For arbitrary measures, the transport plan is a probability measure on the product space.

Definition 6 (Coupling of Measures). Let $\alpha\in\mathcal M_+^1(\mathcal X)$ and $\beta\in\mathcal M_+^1(\mathcal Y)$. A coupling of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ is a probability measure $$ \pi\in\mathcal M_+^1(\mathcal X\times\mathcal Y) $$ whose marginals are $\alpha$ and $\beta$. The set of all such couplings is $$ \Pi(\alpha,\beta) = \left\{ \pi: (\operatorname{proj}_{\mathcal X})_\#\pi=\alpha,\; (\operatorname{proj}_{\mathcal Y})_\#\pi=\beta \right\}. $$

In the discrete case, $$ \pi = \sum_{i,j}P_{ij}\delta_{(x_i,y_j)}. $$ The coefficients $P_{ij}$ contain the transport masses, while the constraints $$ \sum_jP_{ij}=a_i, \qquad \sum_iP_{ij}=b_j $$ ensure that the marginals of $\pi$ are exactly $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

Remark 7 (Why the Joint Distribution Matters). Knowing $\alpha$ and $\beta$ determines only the marginal laws of $X$ and $Y$, not how they are connected. A coupling $$ \pi=\operatorname{Law}(X,Y) $$ specifies this dependence structure. In transport language, $\alpha$ tells us where the mass starts, $\beta$ tells us where it ends, and $\pi$ tells us which source mass is connected to which target mass.

For a given $x$, the conditional distribution $$ \pi(dy\mid x) $$ describes how the mass at $x$ is distributed over target locations. A Monge transport is the special deterministic case $$ \pi(dy\mid x)=\delta_{T(x)}(dy). $$

Definition 8 (Kantorovich Problem). The Kantorovich optimal transport cost is $$ L_c(\alpha,\beta) = \inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\alpha,\beta)} \int_{\mathcal X\times\mathcal Y} c(x,y)\,d\pi(x,y). $$ The feasible set is always nonempty because the independent coupling $$ \alpha\otimes\beta $$ belongs to $\Pi(\alpha,\beta)$. Under standard regularity assumptions on the spaces and cost, an optimal coupling exists.

Remark 9 (Expectation Form). If $$ (X,Y)\sim\pi, $$ then $$ \int_{\mathcal X\times\mathcal Y} c(x,y)\,d\pi(x,y) = \mathbb E_\pi[c(X,Y)]. $$ The marginals determine the two distributions separately; the optimization chooses their optimal dependence structure according to the cost $c$.

Proposition 10 (Monge-induced Coupling). If $T_\#\alpha=\beta$, then the map $$ x\mapsto(x,T(x)) $$ induces the coupling $$ \pi_T = (\operatorname{Id},T)_\#\alpha. $$ Its marginals are $\alpha$ and $\beta$. Its transport cost is the same as the cost of the map: $$ = \int c\,d\pi_T = \int_{\mathcal X}c(x,T(x))\,d\alpha(x). $$

Thus every Monge transport defines a Kantorovich coupling: $$ \{\text{Monge-induced couplings}\} \subseteq \Pi(\alpha,\beta). $$ The converse is generally false because a coupling may split the mass at one $x$ among several values of $y$.

Remark 11 (Monge vs. Kantorovich). The difference can be summarized probabilistically: $$ \text{Monge: }Y=T(X), $$ whereas $$ \text{Kantorovich: }(X,Y)\sim\pi, \qquad X\sim\alpha, \qquad Y\sim\beta. $$ Monge searches over deterministic dependence structures; Kantorovich searches over all couplings. When an optimal Kantorovich coupling is concentrated on the graph $$ \{(x,T(x)):x\in\mathcal X\}, $$ the relaxed solution reduces again to a Monge transport map.
